## Historical Development of AI in Medical Diagnosis
The application of artificial intelligence to medical diagnosis has a rich history spanning over five decades, beginning with pioneering work in the 1970s. The first major breakthrough came with MYCIN, developed at Stanford University in the mid-1970s, which was designed to help physicians diagnose bacteremia and meningitis by asking questions about patient data and laboratory results, then providing both diagnosis and drug therapy recommendations (Diefenbach et al., 2004). This early expert system demonstrated that AI could perform at the level of human experts in specific medical domains and established the foundation for AI's role in clinical decision-making (Diefenbach et al., 2004) (Magrabi et al., 2019).
The broader journey of AI in medicine actually began even earlier, with AI research starting in the 1940s and 1950s, though initial expectations of computers mimicking complex human tasks proved too ambitious given the computational resources available at the time (Varghese, 2020). The field experienced significant evolution from the 1990s to 2010s, when the focus shifted to developing more robust tools and algorithms that could handle the realities of medical practice, including imperfect or missing data and small training datasets that are common in healthcare settings (Chang, 2022).
The last decade has brought renewed interest and dramatic progress, facilitated by the availability of large annotated datasets and modern graphical processing units that can train deeper neural networks more efficiently (Varghese, 2020). This has led to remarkable achievements, with AI systems demonstrating dermatologist-level accuracy in skin cancer classification and cardiologist-level performance in detecting arrhythmia, highlighting AI's potential to match or exceed human expert performance in specific diagnostic tasks (Varghese, 2020) (Talimi et al., 2024).
The evolution of AI diagnostic approaches has also shifted from rule-based expert systems to machine learning methods, as maintaining expert systems with rapidly expanding medical research and data has become unmanageable (Bauer et al., 2021). Despite these technological advances, broad adoption of AI applications within healthcare institutions has not yet occurred due to current limitations in data quality and governance, industry standards, and ethical issues (Bauer et al., 2021). The field continues to grapple with key challenges summarized as the RISE criteria: Regulatory aspects, Interpretability, interoperability, and the need for Structured data and Evidence (Varghese, 2020).

## AI Capabilities and Benefits in Patient-Doctor Diagnostic Conversations
Modern AI systems demonstrate remarkable capabilities in processing and analyzing patient-doctor conversations, with the ability to rapidly sift through billions of data points to support diagnostic decision-making. AI can process huge amounts of data rapidly and discover rules to diagnose diseases, particularly aiding diagnosis in under-resourced areas or by inexperienced physicians while effectively improving diagnostic efficiency and saving time for physicians (Liu, 2020). Recent advances in large language models have shown extraordinary performance across multiple medical domains, including lab result diagnostics and medical literature analysis, enabling AI to assist doctors in tasks such as initial patient assessments, disease diagnosis, and treatment suggestions (Zhou, 2023).
AI applications in conversational diagnosis have demonstrated significant practical improvements in clinical workflows. Research shows that AI systems can achieve a 10% improvement over state-of-the-art baselines in identifying medically relevant contributions to doctor-patient discussions, with downstream benefits of 15%, 105%, and 23% improvements respectively for extracting symptoms, medications, and complaints from nearly 7,000 annotated doctor-patient conversations (Khosla et al., 2020). Natural language processing techniques enable AI to extract and summarize complex medical information, facilitating transcription and summarization of conversations to ensure consultations are more focused and productive (Popa et al., 2024).
The technology addresses several critical challenges in modern healthcare delivery. AI can save time for physicians by organizing vast amounts of information traffic, allowing physicians to focus on meaningful information and concentrate on their primary tasks of listening to, sympathizing with, touching and examining, and healing patients (Oyama et al., 2023). Doctor-patient conversations serve as the foundation of diagnostic medicine, but mounting pressure from escalating patient numbers, lack of access to care, short consultation times, and expedited telemedicine adoption have presented challenges that AI can help address (Johri et al., 2023).
Recent developments in conversational AI demonstrate sophisticated diagnostic capabilities through multi-turn dialogue systems. AI-powered conversation agents can pose multi-round clinical queries to effectively collect the most relevant disease diagnostic information, generating clinical queries that mimic real doctors' conversation style with efficient fluency, professionalism, and safety (Li et al., 2024). Studies show that multimodal dialogue with internal reasoning improves diagnostic F1 scores by 6.5% over non-dialogue settings, with additional improvements of up to 20% when incorporating predictions from diagnostic networks (Sviridov et al., 2025).
AI systems also excel at maintaining current medical knowledge and providing real-time decision support. Unlike humans, AI can easily keep up with the exponentially growing body of medical literature required to make effective diagnoses, allowing it to outperform existing healthcare models (Yan et al., 2024). AI-powered decision support systems can provide real-time alerts and recommendations based on new research findings and treatment guidelines, helping physicians make informed decisions about therapy selection and patient care (Popa et al., 2024). These systems can identify emerging treatment options, potential side effects, and patient-specific recommendations based on medical history and genetic profiles (Popa et al., 2024).
The technology shows particular promise in specialized medical domains and resource-constrained settings. AI applications include robot-assisted surgery, virtual nursing assistance, administrative workflow support, fraud detection, dosage error reduction, clinical trial participant identification, preliminary diagnosis, and automated image diagnosis (Nkurunziza et al., 2022). For example, AI can grade retinal images and determine which diabetic retinopathy patients need timely referral to ophthalmologists, demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity while reducing ophthalmologist workload and improving screening efficiency worldwide (Hao et al., 2022).

## Privacy and Data Security Challenges
The implementation of AI in patient-doctor conversations raises fundamental privacy concerns due to the highly sensitive nature of medical data being processed. Doctor-patient conversations contain deeply personal information that must be carefully protected, with recordings made on digital devices and uploaded to secure servers where they are scrubbed of all identifiable information in accordance with HIPAA privacy rules (Naik et al., 2020). However, patients' physiological data and medical records outsourced to medical cloud systems raise stringent privacy concerns, as patient data is associated with sensitive attributes such as age, gender, epidemic status, physiological data, and clinical results that could be maliciously exploited by network eavesdroppers, untrusted cloud servers, or even healthcare providers (Zhang et al., 2021).
Traditional anonymization and pseudonymization techniques have proven inadequate for protecting patient privacy in AI systems. Conventional techniques of anonymization and pseudonymization have been proven to not sufficiently protect from attacks against privacy, as it is still possible to recreate personally identifiable information by making use of correlated data (Ziller et al., 2020) (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2023). Even when data is anonymized by removing all personally identifiable information and keeping only necessary information to train models, anonymization is not infallible and sophisticated attackers can still reconstruct patient information (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2023).
Privacy-preserving machine learning techniques have emerged as critical solutions to address these challenges. Homomorphic encryption represents a promising privacy-preserving method that allows for mathematical operations on encrypted data without revealing its underlying information, enabling AI analysis while maintaining data confidentiality (Popescu et al., 2021). Secure computing protocols can handle both linearly separable and nonlinear data classification while protecting the privacy of user data and support vectors, ensuring that patient case reports remain confidential during online diagnostic services (Chen et al., 2022).
Federated learning has gained significant attention as a method for collaborative AI development without compromising patient privacy. Federated learning allows for collaborative data utilization without sharing or revealing the data itself, enabling the training of machine learning models locally on the site where the data is stored while only sending model parameter updates to a central server for aggregation (Butt et al., 2023) (Mitrovska et al., 2024). This approach ensures that patient data remains localized with its owner while still enabling the training of AI algorithms on remote data, eliminating the need for direct data sharing (Mitrovska et al., 2024) (Jiang et al., 2024).
Differential privacy provides another crucial layer of protection by adding controlled noise to data and model outputs. Differential privacy offers a formal and quantifiable framework for protecting sensitive information, even in federated learning environments where privacy risks are elevated, by limiting the amount of information that any single data point can contribute to the model (Arasteh et al., 2024). Privacy-enhanced mechanisms using differential privacy can reconstruct medical data through variational autoencoders and add differential privacy noise to resist inference attacks while maintaining high-precision disease diagnosis (Wang et al., 2023).
Real-world implementations demonstrate the practical challenges of balancing privacy protection with diagnostic accuracy. Studies show that while privacy-preserving training yields lower accuracy, it largely does not amplify discrimination against age, sex, or comorbidity, though difficult diagnoses and subgroups suffer stronger performance hits in private training (Arasteh et al., 2023). Research indicates that privacy-preserving training of diagnostic deep learning models is possible with excellent diagnostic accuracy and fairness under challenging realistic circumstances of real-life clinical datasets (Arasteh et al., 2023).
Patient trust and regulatory compliance remain critical factors in the successful deployment of AI diagnostic systems. AI systems must comply with strict regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR, requiring robust encryption, anonymization, and clear consent protocols, with patients needing to be fully informed about how their data will be used, particularly when recordings of doctor-patient conversations are involved (Leong et al., 2024) (Parvin et al., 2025). Enhanced privacy and security measures not only ensure regulatory compliance but build patient trust, which appears to be a critical factor in sustained engagement with AI health systems (Wen et al., 2025). Transparent data practices form the foundation of ethical AI implementation in medicine, with patients deserving clear communication about how their information will be used, protected, and potentially shared (Parvin et al., 2025).

## Multi-Stakeholder Collaboration in AI Healthcare Implementation
The implementation of AI in patient-doctor diagnostic conversations fundamentally requires a collaborative triad involving doctors, patients, and AI systems working together in an iterative diagnostic process. This diagnostic co-design recognizes the mediating role of machine learning systems in clinical decision-making, where the quality of interaction depends on examining how AI simultaneously expands and narrows medical attention, highlighting certain aspects while disclosing others (Kudina et al., 2021). Rather than viewing AI as simply solving existing healthcare concerns, stakeholders must understand how it shapes medical expertise and transforms interactions between doctors and patients (Kudina et al., 2021).
Collaboration between technical and medical experts proves crucial for developing AI tools that are highly acceptable, deployable, and provide real value for patients, doctors, and society. AI-based methods enable detection of diagnostic factors that are unnoticeable to humans, but this capability can only be effectively harnessed through close cooperation between different domains of expertise (Klumpp et al., 2021). Strong collaborations between clinicians and AI researchers are vital for successful implementation, allowing for better understanding of specific healthcare challenges and requirements while ensuring AI models align with clinical practices and standards (Mandala, 2023). These collaborative efforts facilitate the collection of high-quality annotated datasets crucial for training robust and reliable AI models (Mandala, 2023).
Building trust between different stakeholder groups emerges as a fundamental challenge requiring deliberate attention throughout the development process. Medical professionals often hesitate to integrate AI into clinical environments due to concerns about unpredictable system behavior and inability to follow AI logic, making professional feedback incorporation pivotal since these are the people who will ultimately employ the systems (Rong et al., 2022). Trust-building happens throughout model development rather than as a final step, with constant communication needed to overcome obstacles that may divide clinicians and data scientists in collaborations (Henkin et al., 2023). Clinical users and AI developers perceive a strong link between relational and epistemic trust, believing that AI tools earn reliability perceptions over time through use by trusted individuals (Hallowell et al., 2022).
The complexity of healthcare AI implementation demands multidisciplinary teams that bring together diverse expertise and perspectives. Applying AI methods to healthcare problems requires collaborative effort combining clinician expertise, data science knowledge, and other specialists, as knowledge is often dispersed throughout teams and demands both medical domain knowledge and technical AI expertise (Henkin et al., 2023). However, current AI initiatives often suffer from lack of multidisciplinary collaboration, with successful initiatives requiring diverse stakeholder involvement that is frequently hindered by late involvement and challenging communication (Leenen et al., 2025).
Stakeholder collaboration must address the diverse and sometimes conflicting needs of different groups involved in AI healthcare implementation. Patients often seek clear, simple explanations for diagnoses, while physicians require more detailed technical insights, and hospital administrators focus on operational efficiency and cost-effectiveness (Alharthi et al., 2024). Research reveals divergent preferences between clinicians and educators, with educators gravitating towards granular data for tailored interventions while clinicians favor concise formats for efficient decision-making during limited patient interactions (Abdelaal et al., 2024). Clinicians value shared screens to foster patient autonomy and build trust through transparent data sharing (Abdelaal et al., 2024).
Recent collaborative AI frameworks demonstrate the practical benefits of human-AI partnership in diagnostic decision-making. Randomized controlled trials show that clinicians using collaborative AI workflows achieve significantly higher diagnostic accuracies of 85% (AI-first) and 82% (AI-second) compared to 75% with traditional resources, underscoring the value of collaborative systems that complement clinician expertise (Everett et al., 2025). Dynamic human-AI collaboration frameworks enable multi-turn conversational interactions where human expertise and AI-generated insights evolve together, creating safety nets in complex medical scenarios (Sayin et al., 2025).
Successful stakeholder collaboration requires addressing fundamental structural and organizational challenges in healthcare institutions. Hospitals need clear visions for integrating AI, including meeting preconditions in infrastructure and expertise, while stakeholders including IT departments and healthcare professionals often lack required skills and knowledge for effective AI integration in clinical workflows (Leenen et al., 2025). Collaborative initiatives among policymakers, researchers, industry leaders, and patient advocates are essential for developing regulatory guidelines, creating ethical frameworks, and establishing best practices that encourage responsible AI adoption in medical diagnosis (Li et al., 2025).
The integration of multiple stakeholder perspectives must extend beyond technical development to include ongoing governance and quality assurance. Multidisciplinary workforces of clinicians and AI scientists must work together during each important step: model development, validation, deployment, and ongoing model governance (Luo et al., 2024). Post-deployment fairness audits and feedback loops incorporating healthcare professionals and patients are necessary to identify and correct discrepancies between model predictions and real-world outcomes, particularly for underrepresented populations (Guaragnella et al., 2025). This collaborative approach ensures that AI systems remain scientifically valid and ethically responsible while delivering significant health advantages for patients globally (Kumar et al., 2025).

## Trust and Acceptance Challenges
Trust emerges as the fundamental challenge determining whether AI diagnostic systems will be successfully adopted in healthcare, particularly affecting the vulnerable patient population who must rely on these systems for critical health decisions. When it comes to human interaction, trust is one of the important factors influencing the adoption of AI systems, with developing trust being particularly crucial in healthcare because it involves an element of uncertainty and risk for the vulnerable patient (Larasati et al., 2020). The lack of clarity on how the most advanced AI algorithms function creates serious concerns regarding accountability, trust, and social acceptability of AI technologies, concerns that become even more significant when people's wellbeing is at stake in healthcare settings (Larasati et al., 2020).
Despite AI systems achieving medical task performance at or above human levels, patients consistently demonstrate preference for human doctors over AI-powered diagnostic systems. Recent evidence suggests that an AI system is trusted less than a human doctor in medical treatment decisions, with research showing people prefer a human doctor, followed by a human doctor with an AI system, and an AI system alone ranking last (Riedl et al., 2024). Studies reveal that mentioning a doctor uses AI to assist in diagnosis consistently decreased trust and intention to seek help, with trust being highest when AI was explicitly absent and lowest when AI was extensively used (Chen et al., 2024). This pattern remains consistent across age, gender, education, and partisanship, indicating a broad aversion to AI-assisted diagnosis (Chen et al., 2024).
The explainability and transparency of AI systems prove critical for building trust among both patients and healthcare providers. For AI systems to succeed in diagnostic roles, it will not be enough for them to merely provide accurate diagnoses and predictions - they must also provide explanations to both physicians and patients about why diagnoses are made, as without this, accurate diagnoses might otherwise be ignored or rejected (Alam et al., 2021) (Alam et al._1, 2021). AI is often perceived as a "black-box" with no possible way for laymen to understand how the system produces its output, and this lack of understanding could be a major cause of decreased trust since those without AI background knowledge cannot make educated assumptions about systems they need to trust (Rojahn et al., 2023).
Research demonstrates that explanations can significantly improve user satisfaction and trust, particularly during critical diagnostic moments. Studies show that explanation helps improve satisfaction measures during the critical re-diagnosis period, with local justifications having their greatest effect at the point of re-diagnosis, typically preventing significant decline in subjective ratings of trust and satisfaction (Alam et al., 2021). However, poor explanations could reduce a person's inclination to use diagnostic systems, fuel health anxiety, or cause them to seek second opinions from human clinicians, all of which would further burden healthcare systems (Woodcock et al., 2021).
Clinicians express mixed sentiments toward AI-enabled diagnosis, with concerns about alignment between AI algorithms and human clinical reasoning patterns. Although clinician sentiment toward AI is generally positive, the current form of AI assistance may not be linked with efficient decision-making, as AI algorithms are not well aligned with subjective human reasoning in clinical diagnosis (Hah et al., 2021). In quantitative surveys, clinicians reported perceiving current AI assistance as unlikely to enhance diagnostic capability and negatively influencing their overall performance, suggesting that AI-guided decision making is not congruent with the way clinicians typically make diagnostic decisions (Hah et al., 2021).
Trust calibration and appropriate reliance on AI systems present ongoing challenges for healthcare professionals. Research on trust calibration shows that despite the usefulness of AI-based diagnostic decision support systems, overreliance of physicians on AI-generated diagnoses may lead to diagnostic errors (Sakamoto et al., 2024). Studies reveal that the overall accuracy of trust calibration was only 61.5%, and despite correct calibration, diagnostic accuracy was just 54.5%, indicating significant challenges in helping physicians appropriately calibrate their trust in AI recommendations (Sakamoto et al., 2024). This overreliance, often referred to as automation bias, highlights how users tend to trust high-confidence AI outputs without critically assessing them, particularly problematic in critical healthcare domains (Rezaeian et al., 2025).
Patient trust in AI diagnostic systems is heavily influenced by their evaluation of whether these technologies align with core medical values and goals. Patient opinions about healthcare AI are shaped largely by their assessment of the alignment of new AI technologies with the goals and values of medicine, informed by past experiences of illness and healthcare that often involved interactions with other digital technologies (Richardson et al., 2022). The core value of trust in health systems or physicians recurs as patients reflect on their comfort with introducing more advanced but less transparent data-based AI tools, creating complex interplay between patient trust in AI and trust in health systems or physicians (Richardson et al., 2022).
Safety concerns and the need for robust governance frameworks significantly impact trust in AI diagnostic systems. One of the main challenges in AI healthcare application is safety, related to unsafe and incorrect actions and recommendations by AI algorithms, requiring frameworks for implementing and monitoring safety legislation, identifying risks in AI models' activities, and improving human-AI interactions (Davahli et al., 2021). The mistrust of medical AI systems stems from feelings about the systems themselves as well as technology companies developing the programs, with concerns about data privacy, technical issues with gathering high-quality medical data, and technology companies prioritizing profitability over human lives (Rojahn et al., 2023).
Voice-based AI diagnostic systems face particular trust challenges when patients must reveal sensitive information to technological counterparts. Using voice assistants in medical diagnosis contexts draws attention to trust concepts, as patients revealing personal, sensitive information to voice-based systems would need to trust them, raising questions about system trustworthiness and features that might affect patients' willingness to trust them in medical contexts (Wienrich et al., 2021). Recent research indicates that confounding factors influencing patient trust revolve around competency-evaluation, with trust essentially equated with efficacy determined by whether decisions based on AI clinical advice will effectively achieve patient health goals (Du et al., 2024).
Successful trust-building strategies emphasize AI as supportive rather than replacement technology, with transparency and physician oversight remaining crucial. Evidence suggests that redefining AI as a supportive adjunct to professionals rather than standalone solution could promote acceptance, with emphasis on transparency and providing understandable explanations of AI processes being crucial for diminishing skepticism (Tahtali et al., 2024). Studies show preference for using AI in supportive roles such as triage because of efficiency and process optimization capabilities, while there remains discernible hesitancy toward leveraging AI for diagnostic purposes driven by concerns regarding accuracy and the essential nature of human expertise (Tahtali et al., 2024). Research demonstrates that patients may be willing to accept AI in general practice if implemented in ways that preserve the patient-physician relationship and used as support tools for physicians rather than replacements (Mikkelsen et al., 2023).

## Policy Recommendations and Regulatory Frameworks

**Establish comprehensive data governance and privacy frameworks** that ensure patient data protection while enabling AI development. Healthcare AI solutions must align with HIPAA, GDPR, and other privacy regulations, requiring robust encryption, anonymization protocols, and clear consent procedures for patient data use in AI systems (Paranjape, 2021) (Stafie et al., 2023) (Azeem et al., 2023) (Islam, 2024). Policy frameworks should mandate the use of privacy-preserving technologies such as federated learning, differential privacy, and homomorphic encryption to enable collaborative AI development without compromising patient confidentiality (Qiu et al., 2024) (Karajgi et al., 2025) (Koutsoubis et al., 2025).
**Mandate algorithmic transparency and explainability requirements** for AI diagnostic systems used in clinical settings. Healthcare AI applications must provide clear, interpretable explanations for their diagnostic recommendations that can be understood by both healthcare professionals and patients (Zhao et al., 2023) (Sivaprasad et al., 2024) (Olumuyiwa et al., 2024). Regulatory bodies should require AI developers to disclose training data sources, algorithmic decision-making processes, and potential biases to ensure accountability and enable proper clinical validation (Abulibdeh et al., 2025).
**Develop standardized clinical validation and certification processes** for AI diagnostic tools before deployment in healthcare settings. AI systems must undergo rigorous testing across diverse patient populations to ensure safety, efficacy, and fairness (Raman et al., 2021) (Rao et al., 2024) (M et al., 2024). Regulatory frameworks should establish clear guidelines for clinical trials, performance benchmarks, and ongoing monitoring of AI systems post-deployment (Karajgi et al., 2025) (Huang et al., 2025).
**Create clear accountability and liability frameworks** that define responsibility when AI systems are involved in diagnostic decisions. Policy guidelines must address who bears responsibility for diagnostic errors involving AI - whether physicians, AI developers, healthcare institutions, or regulators - and establish clear protocols for error reporting and system improvement (Hill et al., 2023) (Huang et al., 2025) (Wiratchawa et al., 2025). Legal frameworks should emphasize that AI serves as a supportive tool rather than replacement for human medical judgment (Rudnik et al., 2023) (Anderson et al., 2019).
**Implement bias mitigation and equity requirements** to ensure AI diagnostic systems provide fair treatment across all patient populations. Regulatory standards should mandate diverse training datasets, regular bias auditing, and performance monitoring across different demographic groups to prevent healthcare disparities (Raman et al., 2021) (Azeem et al., 2023) (Chinta et al., 2024) (Chinta et al., 2025). Organizations must conduct systematic audits of AI system performance to identify and address emerging biases or inconsistencies that could affect diagnostic accuracy for underrepresented populations (Olumuyiwa et al., 2024) (Bergemann et al., 2024).
**Foster multi-stakeholder collaboration in AI governance and oversight** involving clinicians, AI developers, patients, policymakers, and regulatory bodies. Policy frameworks should require interdisciplinary collaboration throughout the AI development lifecycle, from design to deployment and ongoing monitoring (Haig et al., 2022) (Ennis-O'Connor et al., 2024) (Cabral et al., 2025). Collaborative initiatives among stakeholders are essential for developing ethical frameworks, establishing best practices, and ensuring responsible AI adoption that serves all populations equitably (Paranjape, 2021) (Hudu et al., 2025) (Abulibdeh et al., 2025).
**Establish continuous monitoring and adaptive regulation mechanisms** that can evolve with rapidly advancing AI technologies. Given AI's continuous learning capabilities, regulatory frameworks must include post-market surveillance requirements, performance monitoring systems, and adaptive policies that can respond to emerging risks and opportunities (Abulibdeh et al., 2025) (Huang et al._1, 2025). Healthcare institutions should implement feedback loops between clinicians and AI developers to enable continuous system improvement and safety enhancement (Huang et al._1, 2025).
**Mandate human oversight and clinical decision-making authority** in all AI-assisted diagnostic processes. Policy guidelines should ensure that final diagnostic and treatment decisions remain under human physician control, with AI serving in a supportive capacity (Rudnik et al., 2023) (Olumuyiwa et al., 2024) (Anderson et al., 2019). Regulations should require clear protocols for documenting how AI suggestions influence clinical decisions to maintain transparency and accountability while preserving the essential patient-physician relationship (Wiratchawa et al., 2025) (Anderson et al., 2019).
**Develop integration standards and interoperability requirements** for AI systems within existing healthcare infrastructure. Policy frameworks should establish technical standards that enable seamless integration of AI tools with electronic health records and existing clinical workflows without disrupting patient care (Ennis-O'Connor et al., 2024) (Karajgi et al., 2025). Standards should address data formats, system compatibility, and workflow optimization to maximize AI benefits while minimizing implementation barriers.
**Create education and training requirements** for healthcare professionals using AI diagnostic systems. Regulatory frameworks should mandate appropriate training programs that help clinicians understand AI capabilities, limitations, and proper use in clinical decision-making (Ennis-O'Connor et al., 2024) (Bekbolatova et al., 2024). Public education initiatives should also be implemented to build patient understanding and trust in AI healthcare applications while ensuring informed consent for AI-assisted care (Bekbolatova et al., 2024) (Hoseini, 2023).
